How to Research a Stock Before Buying: A Checklist for Long-Term Investors

A stock chart can show where a price has been. It cannot tell you everything about the business behind it. Before buying shares for a long-term portfolio, build a research note that connects the company, its finances, the price you would pay and the risk you would take. The goal is not to find a guaranteed winner. It is to make a decision you can explain—and revisit when the evidence changes.

1. Define what you are researching

Start with the decision, not a price target. Are you considering ownership of a business over several years, or a trade around an event? Those are different questions. Write down your intended holding period, why the company belongs on your research list and what you still need to learn.

Your investing timeframe and willingness and ability to accept losses matter when deciding how an investment fits your overall plan. A longer horizon is not permission to ignore risk. See Investor.gov’s asset allocation guidance.

2. Explain the business before judging the ticker

Describe what the company sells, who pays for it and why customers might choose it over alternatives. Then ask what could weaken that position: competition, changing demand, costs or dependence on a small group of customers. FINRA’s stock evaluation guide offers a useful starting set of business questions.

Try completing this sentence without a slogan: “This company earns money by ___, and my research question is whether ___ can continue.” If the answer is still vague, that is a reason to keep researching, not to fill the gap with confidence.

3. Go to the company’s filings

For a company that files Form 10-K, start with its latest annual filing, then check subsequent quarterly reports and newer disclosures. Other issuers may use different forms. Record both the filing date and the period covered so you do not mistake an old number for a current one.

In the 10-K, focus first on the Business section, Risk Factors, Management’s Discussion and Analysis, and the financial statements and notes. These connect what the company does with its reported results and management’s explanation. Investor.gov’s guide to reading a 10-K explains the main sections.

Keep a source link beside each important number. A presentation can help orient you, but do not treat a promotional slide or social post as a substitute for the underlying filing. Treat management’s expectations as claims to evaluate, not outcomes already achieved.

4. Separate earnings, cash and financial obligations

Read the statements together. The income statement reports revenue, expenses and profit over a period. The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time. The cash-flow statement separates cash movements into operating, investing and financing activities. Reported profit and cash generation are not interchangeable. The SEC’s financial statement guide explains these distinctions and the role of the notes.

Turn that into questions for your research note:

  • What changed in revenue and profit, and what does management say caused it?
  • How does operating cash flow compare with reported profit over several periods?
  • What borrowing, repayment obligations or other commitments need closer reading?
  • Has the share count changed, and what explains the change?

Compare like periods and check the notes before drawing a conclusion. A single strong quarter is a starting point for investigation, not a complete investment case.

5. Evaluate valuation, not just share price

A lower-priced share is not automatically a cheaper business. One common measure is the price-to-earnings ratio: share price divided by earnings per share. For an illustrative company with a $40 share price and $2 in annual earnings per share, P/E is 20. Those figures are hypothetical, not a stock recommendation.

Use a consistent earnings period and basis when comparing companies. Industry differences matter, and no single ratio establishes fair value. FINRA discusses P/E and other measures in its valuation overview.

Write down what would need to go right for the price you are considering to make sense. Then ask what happens to your conclusion if growth is slower or profitability weaker than you assumed. If the case depends on everything going perfectly, make that dependence visible. Do not turn a spreadsheet output into a certainty.

6. Check portfolio fit and write the bear case

A company can be interesting without being an appropriate addition to your portfolio. Look at what you already own, including overlapping exposure inside funds. Would another position concentrate your risk in the same company, industry or economic driver? Investor.gov explains that diversification includes spreading investments both across and within asset categories. It reduces some risks; it does not eliminate the possibility of losses.

Long-term ownership does not guarantee recovery. A company can fail, and common shareholders can receive nothing in bankruptcy. Review Investor.gov’s stock risk overview before treating “I can hold it” as a risk plan.

Before making a decision, write the strongest reason your thesis could be wrong. Identify evidence that would make you reassess it: a deteriorating operating trend, a financing problem or a change in the business you thought you understood. A review trigger is a prompt to investigate, not an automatic buy or sell instruction.

Keep a one-page research record

Use the same short record for each company so your reasoning is easier to compare:

  • Question: What am I trying to decide, and over what timeframe?
  • Evidence: Which dated filings and figures support my view?
  • Assumptions: What am I expecting that has not happened yet?
  • Counterargument: What is the strongest case against my view?
  • Portfolio fit: Which existing risks would this add to?
  • Next review: What new information am I waiting for?

Separate facts, assumptions and unresolved questions. “I do not have enough evidence yet” is a valid research outcome. Saving the note also gives you a record of what you believed before the result was known.

Frequently asked questions

What should I research first before buying a stock?

Start with the business and your reason for considering it. Then connect the latest company filings to the finances, valuation assumptions, downside risks and your existing portfolio. Keep the source dates in your notes.

Does a low P/E mean a stock is undervalued?

Not by itself. A ratio does not explain the quality or durability of the earnings behind it. Investigate the business and the assumptions behind the comparison before drawing a valuation conclusion.

Can AI replace reading company filings?

No. An AI summary is something to check, not the underlying evidence. Open the source, confirm the reporting period and verify the numbers or claims that would affect your decision.
